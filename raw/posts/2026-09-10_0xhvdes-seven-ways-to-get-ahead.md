# 7 Ways to Get Ahead That Most People Ignore

Two people can start from almost the same place. Same age. Similar intelligence. Same 24 hours in a day. Five years later, their lives can look completely different.

One has built valuable skills, a strong network, a reputation, and opportunities that seem to appear out of nowhere.

The other has worked just as hard, but somehow hasn't moved nearly as far. Five years passed, but very little actually changed.

It's easy to explain the difference with luck, talent, or discipline. But that's only part of the story. Some actions simply move you further than others.

One conversation can introduce you to your future business partner. One skill can completely change what you're able to earn. One article can put your work in front of a million people. One small project can turn into a company.

None of these outcomes are guaranteed.

But they all have something in common. The potential upside is much bigger than the effort required to try.

That's one of the biggest differences between people who move ahead quickly and those who stay in roughly the same place.

They don't just work hard. They get better at recognizing where their time, skills, relationships, and effort can create disproportionate returns.

And once you understand that, getting ahead becomes less about doing more and more about doing the right things.

That's what this article is about.

## I. Seek Asymmetric Opportunities

Not all opportunities are created equal. Some have a very predictable exchange:

Work one hour, get paid for one hour.

Do twice the work, get roughly twice the result.

Others work completely differently.

You can spend five hours writing an article and get 500 views.

Or the same five hours can produce an article that reaches a million people, brings thousands of followers, and puts your work in front of someone who later becomes a client.

The effort barely changed. The range of possible outcomes did.

This is asymmetry. Situations where what you can gain is disproportionately larger than what you have to risk.

Venture capital works on a similar principle. An investor can lose at most the money invested in a startup, but a rare winner can return many times that amount. That's why a portfolio doesn't require every company to succeed. A small number of exceptional outcomes can offset many failures.

Your career has asymmetric opportunities too.

Publishing your work.

Starting a small business.

Learning an emerging skill early.

Reaching out to someone far ahead of you.

Moving into a growing industry.

Building something on the side.

Most attempts won't change your life. But importantly, they don't have to.

If ten experiments cost you a few weekends and nine go nowhere, the tenth can still make the entire equation worth it. That's the advantage of playing games where being wrong is cheap, but being right can change your trajectory. Compare that with spending years optimizing something with a tightly capped upside. You can execute perfectly and still only move so far.

You don't need extraordinary results from everything you do. You need enough exposure to things capable of producing extraordinary results.

## II. Build Leverage

For most of human history, there was a fairly tight relationship between how much you worked and how much you could produce.

A farmer could only work so much land.

A craftsman could only make so many products.

A teacher could only teach the people who fit in a room.

The internet changed that.

Today, the same hour of work can produce radically different amounts of value depending on what you build with it.

A developer can write software once and have a million people use it.

A creator can make one video and reach more people than a television network could reach decades ago.

An author can write a book once and sell it for decades.

A founder can build a system that continues operating while they're asleep.

This is leverage. Using something beyond your own time to multiply the output of your effort.

Code is leverage. Content is leverage. Capital is leverage. Products, systems, distribution, and teams can all be leverage. Without leverage, your output is limited by your time. Stop working, and the output stops too.

With leverage, the relationship starts to break.

One hour can create ten hours of value. One decision can affect thousands of customers. One piece of work can continue producing results years after you made it.

This is why two people can work equally hard and get completely different results. One keeps selling their hours. The other spends some of those hours building things that multiply them.

## III. Get Closer to Opportunity

Opportunity isn't evenly distributed.

Some cities produce more startups. Some companies produce more founders. Some communities produce more collaborations, investments, ideas, and careers. Silicon Valley is an obvious example.

For decades, ambitious founders moved there even though companies could technically be built elsewhere. The value wasn't only the location itself. It was the concentration of engineers, investors, founders, talent, knowledge, and capital.

Put enough ambitious people in the same environment and opportunities begin colliding.

One conversation becomes an introduction. An introduction becomes a job. A side project becomes a startup. Someone else's idea changes what you decide to build.

The same thing happens on a much smaller scale.

Spend your time around twenty people building companies, and you'll constantly hear about new tools, industries, jobs, customers, and problems worth solving.

Spend it around twenty people who aren't interested in any of those things, and many of the same opportunities may never even enter your field of view.

The internet has made proximity less geographic than it used to be. A niche X community, group chat, Discord, startup, or online network can put you around people you would have had almost no chance of meeting twenty years ago.

And this creates an important advantage. You can't act on an opportunity you never encounter.

Getting ahead therefore isn't only about becoming more capable. It's also about putting yourself in environments where useful information, ambitious people, and unexpected opportunities pass through more often.

Sometimes changing your trajectory doesn't require working harder. It requires getting closer to where things are happening.

## IV. Become Easy to Bet On

A surprising number of opportunities come from someone deciding to take a chance on you.

A founder hires you.

A client gives you a bigger project.

An investor writes a check. Someone recommends you to a friend. A person you've met twice introduces you to someone important.

In every case, they're making a small bet. And when people make bets, they look for evidence. Imagine two developers with roughly the same level of skill.

One has spent years quietly getting better.

The other built 3 products, contributed to open-source projects, written about what they've learned, and has people willing to recommend them.

They might be equally capable. But they're not equally easy to bet on. The second person has reduced uncertainty.

You can see what they've built. You can see how they think. You can see that they finish things. Other people can verify that they're good. This is one reason reputation becomes so valuable over time.

Every successful project, recommendation, useful piece of work, and promise you keep becomes another piece of evidence about what someone can expect from you.

Eventually, people don't have to take as much of a risk. Your previous work starts making the case for you.

That's also why quietly becoming good at something isn't always enough. The world can't reward skills it doesn't know you have.

Build things.

Publish your work.

Keep your promises.

Help people.

Develop a track record that makes the next person feel comfortable putting their name, money, or opportunity behind you.

Being good creates value. Making it easy for others to see that you're good creates opportunity.

## V. Play Games That Compound

Some games make you start from zero every morning. You do the work, get the result, and tomorrow you have to do roughly the same thing again.

Other games are different.

Yesterday's work makes today's work more valuable. An audience works this way. Your first article starts with nobody reading. Years later, you can publish the same quality of article to hundreds of thousands of people.

Knowledge works this way too. The more you understand, the easier it becomes to connect and understand new ideas.

Relationships compound.

Reputation compounds.

Capital compounds.

Skills compound.

The interesting part is that compounding doesn't only increase what you have. It changes how easily you can get more of it.

A strong reputation makes the next introduction easier.

A larger audience makes the next piece of content easier to distribute.

More knowledge makes the next skill easier to learn. More capital creates more opportunities to invest.

The advantage begins feeding itself.

This is why two people making similar amounts of effort can slowly move further and further apart. One person's work disappears when it's finished. The other's becomes an asset that makes future work more effective.

At first, the difference can look tiny. Over enough time, it becomes difficult to catch. The best games get easier to win the longer you play them.

## VI. Combine Skills Instead of Competing

You probably won't become the best writer in the world. You probably won't become the best marketer, salesperson, designer, or programmer either.

Fortunately, you don't need to.

Scott Adams popularized the idea of a talent stack.

Instead of becoming world-class at one thing, you can become good at several useful things whose combination is unusually valuable.

Imagine you're in the top 10% at writing. That's useful, but there are plenty of great writers.

Now add marketing.

Then sales.

Then an understanding of AI.

Then the ability to build an audience and distribute your ideas.

Individually, none of those skills makes you particularly rare. But finding someone who can do all five becomes much harder.

The combination becomes the advantage. This is especially powerful because skills can multiply each other rather than simply add together.

A developer who understands sales can build things people actually want. A founder who can write can attract customers, employees, and investors. A designer who understands psychology can make products easier to understand and use.

Each additional skill changes what you can do with the ones you already have. And this creates another way to get ahead.

Instead of entering the most competitive game and trying to become number one, you can create a combination where there are far fewer people to compete with.

You don't always need to become the best. Sometimes you need to become difficult to compare.

## VII. Increase Your Surface Area for Luck

Luck is random. But your exposure to luck isn't.

Every time you publish something, meet someone new, build a project, enter a new community, or try an experiment, you create another opportunity for something unexpected to happen. Most of these attempts will lead nowhere.

But that's the point. You don't need all of them to work. You need enough exposure for a few unusually valuable opportunities to eventually find you.

From the outside, those moments often look like luck. What you don't see are all the actions that increased the probability of that luck happening.

I've written an entire article about How to Increase Your Luck., so I won't go too deep into it here.

The principle is simple:

You can't manufacture luck on command. But you can give luck more chances to find you.

Now here's what I actually want you to take away from all of this.

Getting ahead isn't about working harder than everyone around you. It's about putting your effort in places where it can create disproportionate returns. You don't need every decision to change your life.

You just need a few that change your trajectory. ❤️
