# The End of an Era: What the UAE's OPEC Exit Means for the World

## Original Content

On the morning of April 28, 2026, the United Arab Emirates announced it would leave the OPEC effective May 1 2026 ending 59 year old arrangement. 'The time has come to focus our efforts on what our national interest dictates,' Abu Dhabi's energy ministry said. In those fourteen words lay the quiet obituary of the post-1973 oil order.

Before the Tehran Agreement, Western oil companies - the "Seven Sisters" unilaterally set the posted price of oil and paid Gulf governments a fixed royalty and tax on that price. The governments had almost no negotiating power individually. OPEC gave them collective bargaining power, and the Tehran Agreement was the first time that collective power produced a concrete, measurable financial result: a ~30% immediate increase in per-barrel government revenue.

### The Architecture That Is Breaking

The global oil system that emerged from the 1970s rested on three interlocking pillars.

- **OPEC price coordination:** member states agreed to suppress their individual production ambitions in exchange for collectively higher prices -a cartel logic that, when it worked, transferred extraordinary wealth from consuming nations to producing ones.
- **The Petrodollar:** by pricing oil exclusively in US dollars, Gulf producers sustained global demand for American currency, recycling their surpluses into US Treasuries and underpinning the dollar's status as the world's reserve currency.
- **American defence:** Washington provided the military umbrella - fleets in the Persian Gulf, bases across the Arabian Peninsula that made the entire arrangement feel permanent.

All three pillars are now under simultaneous stress, and the UAE's departure is the most visible crack in the first.

### The Largest Defection in OPEC History

The UAE's exit is categorically different from every prior departure. Qatar left in 2019 but produced almost entirely natural gas, so its exit barely registered in crude markets. Angola left in 2024 citing quota disputes, and markets flinched only briefly. The UAE, by contrast, is OPEC's third-largest producer, with a current output capacity of approximately 3.5 million barrels per day (30% less than actual capacity) and the explicit ambition, backed by $150 billion in investment through its national oil company ADNOC, to reach 5 million barrels per day by 2027.

More critically, the UAE was one of only two OPEC members -the other being Saudi Arabia with meaningful spare production capacity: the idle barrels that can be turned on quickly to absorb a supply shock or defend a price floor. Spare capacity is the cartel's actual instrument of market power. Saudi Arabia and the UAE together controlled a majority of the world's total spare capacity of more than 4 million barrels per day. Without the UAE's contribution, OPEC's ability to respond to price crises is fundamentally diminished.

For years, Abu Dhabi had watched fellow members game the system. Iraq and Russia routinely exceeded their assigned quotas with minimal consequence while the UAE complied and left money on the table. Baker Institute researchers estimated that unconstrained production could generate Abu Dhabi upwards of $50 billion in additional annual revenues.

### The Oligopoly Demand Curve

OPEC operated almost precisely as the kinked demand curve model predicts. The cartel held price at a collectively agreed level — the equivalent of P in this chart. Any member that tried to raise prices above the cartel level lost market share because buyers went elsewhere. Any member that cut prices below it gained almost nothing because other members immediately matched the cut. The rational strategy was to hold at P, Q and not deviate. The kink kept everyone in place.

The spare capacity that Saudi Arabia and the UAE held was the enforcement mechanism. It was the threat that made the kink real — if anyone defected from the agreed price, the Saudis could flood the market and destroy the price-cutter's revenue.

When the United Arab Emirates steps away, one key thing changes: the spare oil capacity it held — about 1.5 million barrels per day — is no longer under the control of OPEC. Earlier, this extra capacity acted like a powerful deterrent: if any country tried to produce more than agreed, OPEC could quickly release this oil and push prices down, keeping everyone in line.

Without that backup, the group's ability to enforce discipline weakens. Countries are more likely to test limits, and supply becomes harder to control tightly. ### The Iran War: Catalyst, Not Cause

The immediate trigger was the Iran war. When Tehran began attacking shipping in the Strait of Hormuz through which a fifth of the world's crude oil normally passes — the UAE found itself effectively at war with a fellow OPEC member. Missile and drone strikes targeted UAE infrastructure for weeks. The UAE's production slumped 44% to around 1.9 million barrels per day following the Hormuz closure, threatening the foundation of its economy. Iran, simultaneously an OPEC partner in the institutional sense and an active military adversary in the practical one, made continued membership not just economically costly but politically untenable.

### Emergency Swap Lines from US

The war also revealed a deeper geopolitical realignment already years in the making. The UAE had signed the Abraham Accords with Israel in 2020. It had deepened security and economic ties with the United States. When the Hormuz crisis erupted, it was Washington not Riyadh that offered Abu Dhabi emergency dollar swap lines, publicly backed before the US Senate by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent in the days immediately preceding the OPEC announcement.

### UAE vs. Saudi Arabia: The Rivalry Beneath the Rupture

For decades, the relationship between Abu Dhabi and Riyadh was framed as an alliance - two Gulf monarchies united by family ties, shared security concerns, and OPEC membership. That framing has become increasingly fictional. Once firm allies, the two countries have developed a simmering rivalry covering oil policy, regional geopolitics, and the fierce competition to attract foreign talent, capital, and global prestige.

The disagreements predated the Iran war. The UAE and Saudi Arabia clashed repeatedly over OPEC production baselines - the reference points used to calculate each member's quota. Outside OPEC, the two countries backed opposing sides in Yemen's civil war, ultimately leading to Saudi Arabia bombing what it described as a UAE-backed weapons shipment in late December — a rupture that a Gulf official told Reuters created a "huge trust gap that might never be repaired."

Economically, Dubai and Abu Dhabi have positioned themselves as direct competitors to Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 ambitions. Both countries are racing to diversify their economies, attract international business headquarters, build tourism infrastructure, and become global financial hubs. In the race for international capital, they are rivals as often as they are partners.

### The Petrodollar's Long Goodbye

To understand why the petrodollar system is now fracturing, you have to understand what it actually was — and most accounts get this fundamentally wrong.

The petrodollar is almost universally described as an oil-for-dollars arrangement. Gulf producers price oil in dollars. Dollars flow back to the United States as Treasury purchases. American borrowing costs stay low. The cycle repeats. That description is accurate as far as it goes, but it describes the plumbing. It does not describe the foundation. The foundation was never the dollar. The foundation was always **defence**.

Go back to 1974. Saudi Arabia had just orchestrated the most successful economic embargo in modern history, quadrupling oil prices and bringing the Western industrial economies to their knees. King Faisal was, on paper, the most powerful oil sovereign on earth. And yet he was also a man who understood, with absolute clarity, the fragility of his position. Saudi Arabia was a sparsely populated kingdom sitting atop the world's largest hydrocarbon reserves in the most violent neighbourhood on the planet. It had no credible conventional military.

What Faisal needed from the United States was not a currency arrangement. It was a guarantee. A commitment, explicit or implicit, that the world's most powerful military would stand between the House of Saud and everything that threatened it. What Henry Kissinger engineered between 1974 and 1975 was precisely that — not a contract, not a treaty, but what one historian described as "a web of mutual interests": American military protection for Saudi Arabia's oil fields, arms sales worth billions of dollars, intelligence cooperation, and the unspoken but universally understood commitment that Washington would not allow the Kingdom to fall. In exchange, Saudi Arabia priced its oil in dollars, invested its surpluses in US Treasuries, and used its dominance inside OPEC to ensure the rest of the membership followed suit.

The dollar pricing was the **rent Saudi Arabia paid for its security guarantee**. The petrodollar system was, at its origin and in its essence, a protection arrangement — the most consequential and longest-running protection arrangement in the history of the modern world. The dollar was not the cause. It was the consequence.

### Saudi Arabia's Anxiety and the Pakistan Hedge

Saudi Arabia began showing signs of anxiety about the security guarantee long before the Iran war. It watched the United States withdraw from Afghanistan in 2021. It watched Washington's pivot away from the Middle East accelerate under successive administrations. It watched Iranian drones strike Saudi Aramco facilities in 2019 and the American response amount to strongly-worded statements. The implicit guarantee that had structured Saudi foreign policy for fifty years was starting to feel like something that could not be relied upon unconditionally. And so Riyadh began hedging — not loudly, not formally, but systematically.

The Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement with Pakistan, in September 2025, was the most visible expression of that hedge. On its face it was a straightforward mutual defence commitment: any aggression against one country would be treated as aggression against both. Saudi Arabia was not abandoning the American umbrella. It was trying to build a second one alongside it, a backup guarantee for the scenarios it feared Washington might not show up for.

Pakistan's role quickly became contradictory. When the Iran war began on Feb 28, 2026, it sent fighter jets to support Saudi Arabia — but at the same time, it was hosting high-level talks between United States and Iran. So Pakistan was both a defence partner to Saudi and a mediator for Iran. It expected financial rewards from this balancing act, but didn't receive them — and the deterrence strategy failed.

### Fragmentation of the Security Guarantee

The petrodollar system is not dying because producers have fallen out of love with the dollar. It is dying because the security guarantee that made Gulf producers choose the dollar in the first place is fragmenting — from a single multilateral American umbrella into a series of bilateral arrangements, each negotiated separately, each carrying different terms, and each carrying a different implicit answer to the question that has always sat at the centre of Gulf politics: *if someone comes for us, who will come for us in return?*

### What This Means for India

Of all the major oil-importing economies, India sits in arguably the most precarious position. Nearly 85% of oil is imported in India. India is not simply a price-taker in global oil markets; it is specifically and structurally dependent on the precise type of oil that the Gulf produces.

On the challenging side, the fragmentation of OPEC's coordination mechanism increases the volatility India must manage. India's central bank and finance ministry have historically benefited from a degree of predictability in oil prices — even when those prices were elevated — that allowed reasonably accurate budget planning. In a world where prices swing more sharply and the cartel's stabilizing function is weakened, India's import bill becomes harder to forecast and its current account deficit more exposed to sudden deterioration — and therefore, weakening of Indian rupee.

However, it is said that UAE might route more crude to India, using the Habshan-Fujairah oil pipeline, bypassing the Strait of Hormuz.

### A Precedent That Will Be Watched Closely

Abu Dhabi did not consult Riyadh before leaving. It did not raise the issue with any OPEC member. It made a unilateral sovereign decision, announced it publicly, and set a three-day implementation timeline. That process — and its apparent absence of consequences — will be studied carefully by every other OPEC member sitting on unproduced oil and irritated under quota constraints.

Each departure has lowered the institutional cost of the next one. Gulf OPEC delegates have already privately warned that the exit could spur further defections, as several members have long chafed under Saudi dominance. The logic is now unavoidable: if the UAE can leave and pump freely, the incentive for staying inside the cartel and deferring to Riyadh erodes fast.

The remaining members most likely to follow the UAE's logic are those with spare capacity, investment ambition, and diplomatic flexibility:
- **Kuwait:** with its underinvested oil sector and long-standing quota frustrations
- **Iraq:** which has chronically overproduced relative to its targets
- **Nigeria:** which has been rebuilding production capacity after years of infrastructure decay

None of these exits is imminent. But the template is now established: a credible, capable producer can leave OPEC, frame it as a national interest decision, secure bilateral relationships with consuming nations and Western financial institutions, and face no obvious punishment.

### Short-Term and Medium-Term Pricing

**Short-term:** The short-term oil price story is being written by the Strait of Hormuz, not by OPEC membership. With Brent crude trading between $108 and $110 per barrel and WTI above $105, the market is pricing in the physical impossibility of moving Gulf barrels. The UAE's exit from OPEC, however momentous institutionally, changes nothing about that physical reality. The UAE cannot produce what it cannot ship.

**Medium-term:** When the Strait of Hormuz reopens — whether through a ceasefire, a naval intervention, or a negotiated settlement that the UAE has inserted itself into as a condition-setter — several variables converge simultaneously:
- UAE production will ramp toward 5 million barrels per day unconstrained by any quota
- Saudi Arabia will face a weaker cartel and a more competitive Gulf peer with lower fiscal breakeven costs

The arithmetic of a post-Hormuz reopening, under these conditions, points firmly toward **lower prices in the medium term**.
