# Do Less

Earlier this year I went to a sleep doctor because I couldn't sleep. I thought I had sleep apnoea, or something else wrong. Instead, I came away realising how stressed I was, and the curse that being constantly productive had placed on my health.

Last week I wrote an article about [how choosing the right game is harder than winning it](https://x.com/finlayekins/status/2100362862388785332). It missed a crucial part: you need enough mental space to think about what you want in the first place.

When every spare moment is filled with work, scrolling, exercise or seeing friends, we lose that space. We spend the day doing things, feeling productive, and never face the bigger question of whether any of it matters. It's why so many of our best thoughts come in the shower, on a plane, or when we're in bed - when we genuinely have no other inputs.

## Stress Breeds Stress

Every time I travel and fully switch off, it takes me about four days to feel relaxed. For the first few I feel anxious and unproductive. I want to find something to do so I can feel useful again.

I recently came across [stress habituation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9797525/). In one study, people reported feeling less stressed with repeated exposure, even though their physical stress response didn't fall in the same way. Another cool term: [allostatic load](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10681886/), the wear and tear on your body from repeatedly adapting to stress. You can stop noticing the stress while still damaging yourself longterm.

In [a series of experiments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4330241/), people generally didn't enjoy spending even 6 to 15 minutes alone with nothing to do but think. They preferred mundane activities. I grab my phone out of my pocket habitually with even 30 seconds of spare time.

It's so easy to satisfy now. There's always a screen, another message, another thing to do. Social media gives you something to fill the smallest gap. Busyness does the same thing while letting you feel productive.

I stopped using Instagram and use Opal to block my screen time because otherwise my brain fills those gaps automatically. I still jump into busywork, and I'm not great at sitting with myself or meditating. But you can feel the difference when I give my thoughts a bit of room.

## Focus Matters Everywhere

People talk about relentless focus as a way to build a successful business. I think it's more important as a life skill. The world keeps getting faster and dopamine keeps getting easier to reach. Being able to block it out and do something you actually care about, even for 30 minutes, is becoming rare. Especially when that thing has no obvious payoff.

There's also a reason to leave your mind space. In [one experiment](https://www.cmhp.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/2018-12/Baird%20et%20al.%20%282012%29%20Inspired%20by%20distraction.pdf), people came back to a creative problem with more ideas after an undemanding task than after a demanding task or no break. Being occupied every second doesn't necessarily produce your best thinking.

I want time to think about the bigger picture, to let creative thoughts come back, and to ask the questions I can avoid by staying busy. That applies to the work I choose, but also to the life around it.

## The Work Version

AI makes it absurdly easy to act on every work impulse. I audited one day in Claude Code and found 23 sessions across seven projects and 98 commits. None of it was revenue work. I could point to those numbesr, but I'd spent the day switching between things rather than choosing what mattered.

*We recently decided to stop setting up personal agents for three recruiters. It was work we could do, and money we could say yes to. I found myself asking: what's the point of doing it for three people? We were rushing towards a revenue goal and filling our time with work that pulled us away from the products we want to build. And because our focus was elsewhere, we weren't delivering our best work either.*

That's what doing less looks like for us right now: sometimes cutting a project, not finding a more efficient way to keep it alive. AI can give us time back, but we can fill it again immediately if we're not careful.

I don't think there's a clear answer. I want to do good, creative work without being in a constant stressed state. Stressful moments are inevitable, but I'm trying to do things with the minimum viable stress required to get them done. Easier said than done when being stressed can make you feel productive.

## Some Things to Try

Things that have helped me return to a creative baseline:

1. **Pick just three things for today.** On Monday I put everything that needs doing that week on a yellow notepad. Each morning I pick only three and copy them onto a second pad. Commit to leaving the rest behind.
2. **Timebox something and start it.** Get rid of all distractions, set a 30 minute timer, and do the thing at the top of your list.
3. **Leave empty calendar blocks.** Don't immediately turn a free hour into admin, or say yes to that coffee. Protect space to do nothing with your life.
4. **Keep something you don't optimise.** A hobby, a walk, time with friends. Something you do because you enjoy it, not because it might make you better at work.
5. **Audit your week, then cut something.** List what you did, circle what mattered, then find one recurring thing you could stop entirely.
6. **Make the distractions harder to reach.** I stopped using Instagram and use Opal to block the apps I open without thinking. On the extreme end, try a flip phone for a week - life changing. Apps like SociaLite also block the distracting parts from apps (i.e. Instagram Reels).
