# Why China Got Rich and India Didn't

An influential theory called "state capacity" claims that effective governments are the key to economic growth. Last week, one of the theory's leading scholarly exponents, the economist James Robinson, published a [piece](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/fandd/issues/Series/Analytical-Series/state-capacity-and-development-robinson) arguing that American economic success rests on the tried-and-true administrative state, and leftists should therefore embrace it. Historically, the argument goes, state capacity determines who grows — more capable states create more wealth.

The problem is that this theory can't explain the economic trajectories of China and India over the last several decades.

In the 1980s, China had what Robinson himself described as an "omnipotent" state. For the last 3,000 years, the Chinese state was one of the most powerful and centralized in world history. In contrast, modern India's state has always been famously weak and fractured. Democracies, as Robinson understands, are generally less capable than autocracies. India's state capacity rating, assigned by statistical agencies, has persistently fallen below China's.

And yet, despite these differences in state capacity, the two countries have swapped positions on the global economic hierarchy over the last forty years. In 1980, India's GDP per capita was more than 70% higher than China's. Today, China's GDP per capita is more than 200% higher than India's.

State capacity theory would expect the country with the historically more capable state — China — to have been richer already in 1980, but it was actually poorer. State capacity theory would expect India's growth to have accelerated as its state became more capable over time, but instead it has consistently underperformed. There's effectively no correlation between state capacity and economic outcomes in this crucial comparison. Both countries built up state capacity, but only one got rich.

This empirical puzzle points to a deeper conceptual problem. State capacity theory tacitly assumes that output is a function of inputs: more and better state institutions produce more economic growth. But as the economist Albert Hirschman pointed out, development isn't primarily about combining factors of production. It's about mobilizing and organizing resources that already exist.

Effective states are not a prerequisite for growth; they are often its product. When societies find ways to organize themselves around shared economic projects, they generate state capacity as a kind of byproduct. The relationship isn't causal in the straightforward direction that state capacity theory assumes. It's dialectical: new forms of coordination create new institutional capabilities, which in turn enable further coordination.

China's economic takeoff was not the result of a competent state implementing wise policies. It was the result of the Chinese state losing control — deliberately and partially — over key areas of economic life, particularly in the countryside. The decollectivization of agriculture and the Township and Village Enterprises (TVEs) of the 1980s and 1990s weren't examples of the state directing economic activity. They were examples of the state allowing economic activity to happen outside its direct control.

This is precisely what India has struggled to do. The Indian state, despite its weakness in absolute terms, maintains a sprawling web of regulations, permits, and restrictions — the famous "License Raj" — that prevents the kind of bottom-up economic organization that drove Chinese growth. Indian entrepreneurs must navigate a labyrinth of bureaucratic approvals just to start businesses, expand factories, or hire workers. The state is too weak to provide effective public goods like infrastructure and education, but strong enough to block private initiative.

The concept of state capacity lumps together two very different things: the ability to *enable* and the ability to *control*. Historically, the most successful developing states have been those that learned to enable while deliberately limiting their control functions. The developmental state's vital role was not comprehensive planning but targeted catch-up: channeling resources into strategic sectors, protecting infant industries, and coordinating investment decisions that markets wouldn't make on their own.

What explains the divergence, then, comes down to the political settlement — the social coalition capable of pursuing a shared economic project. China's post-Mao settlement allowed a broad coalition to form around economic growth as the central goal, freeing up local initiative through what researchers call "regionally decentralized authoritarianism." Local officials had incentives to promote growth, and rural populations had opportunities to organize production outside state planning. India's political settlement, by contrast, has never achieved this kind of growth-centered coalition across its diverse federal structure. Instead, political energy is consumed by distributional conflicts among caste groups, linguistic communities, and regional interests. Growth has been an outcome rather than an organizing objective.

This distinction between enablement and control doesn't just help explain the China-India puzzle. It also illuminates America's own economic history, contra Robinson. The United States developed less through capable administration from Washington and more through a *permissive federal structure* — one that allowed states and municipalities to experiment, compete, borrow, and even default. The American developmental state, to the extent that one existed, was decentralized and permissionless before it was centralized and administrative. The Erie Canal, the transcontinental railroads, the electrical grid — these were not products of federal planning but of state-granted charters, municipal bonds, and private-public hybrid corporations operating in a legal environment that made ambitious projects possible. The administrative state came later, largely to manage the externalities and instabilities that the earlier developmental phase had created.

State capacity theory, in other words, mistakes the map for the territory. It observes that rich countries have capable states and concludes that capable states make countries rich. But political settlements — not administrative capability — are the actual driving force. The state is the terrain on which coalitions operate, not the motive force itself.

For developing countries today, the lesson is counterintuitive but clear: the path to prosperity runs not through building more capable states but through achieving political settlements that allow economic energy to be unleashed. State capacity follows growth, not the other way around. China got rich not because it had a strong state, but because it had a political settlement capable of letting go.
