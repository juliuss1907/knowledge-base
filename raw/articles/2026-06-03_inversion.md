Charlie Munger, the business partner of Warren Buffett and Vice Chairman of Berkshire Hathaway, is famous for his quote "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there." That thinking was inspired by the German mathematician Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi, famous for some work on elliptic functions. Jacobi often solved difficult problems by following a simple strategy: "man muss immer umkehren" (or loosely translated, "invert, always invert.")

> "[Jacobi] knew that it is in the nature of things that many hard problems are best solved when they are addressed backward," Munger counsels.

While Jacobi applied inversion mostly to mathematics, the model is one of the most powerful mental models in our toolkit.

It is not enough to think about difficult problems one way. You need to think about them forwards and backward. Inversion often forces you to uncover hidden beliefs about the problem you are trying to solve. "Indeed," says Munger, "many problems can't be solved forward."

Let's take a look at some examples. Say you want to improve innovation in your organization. Thinking forward, you'd think about all of the things you could do to foster innovation. If you look at the problem by inversion, however, you'd think about all the things you could do that would discourage innovation. Ideally, you'd avoid those things. Sounds simple right? I bet your organization does some of those 'stupid' things today.

Another example, rather than think about what makes a good life, you can think about what prescriptions would ensure misery.

## Avoiding stupidity is easier than seeking brilliance.

While both thinking forward and thinking backward result in some action, you can think of them as additive vs. subtractive.

Despite our best intentions, thinking forward increases the odds that you'll cause harm (iatrogenics). Thinking backward, call it subtractive avoidance or inversion, is less likely to cause harm.

Inverting the problem won't always solve it, but it will help you avoid trouble. You can think of it as the avoiding stupidity filter. It's not sexy but it's a very easy way to improve.

So what does this mean in practice?

Spending time thinking about the opposite of what you want doesn't come naturally to most people. And yet many of the smartest people in history, have done this naturally.

Inversion helps improve understanding of the problem. By forcing you to do the work necessary to have an opinion you're forced to consider different perspectives.

If you're to take anything away from inversion let it be this: Spend less time trying to be brilliant and more time trying to avoid obvious stupidity. The kicker? Avoiding stupidity is easier than seeking brilliance.

## Practical Applications of Inversion

### Avoiding Failure in Business

Instead of asking "How can we grow revenue?", ask "What would guarantee revenue decline?" The answers might include: ignore customer feedback, stop innovating, cut marketing spend, hire the wrong people, lose focus on quality. By identifying these failure modes, you can actively avoid them.

### Avoiding Failure in Investing

Rather than asking "What stocks will go up?", ask "What stocks will definitely lose money?" The answer might include: companies with massive debt, no competitive advantage, incompetent management, or in declining industries. By avoiding these, you improve your odds.

### Avoiding Failure in Relationships

Instead of asking "What makes a good marriage?", ask "What ruins marriages?" The answers include: lack of communication, dishonesty, taking your partner for granted, not making time for each other. Avoid these and you're more likely to succeed.

### Avoiding Failure in Health

Rather than asking "How do I get healthy?", ask "How do I guarantee poor health?" The answers: eat processed foods, never exercise, sleep poorly, ignore stress, avoid doctors. Invert these and you have a simple health plan: eat whole foods, move regularly, prioritize sleep, manage stress, get checkups.

## The Stoics and Inversion

The Stoic philosophers used a technique called *premeditatio malorum* — the premeditation of evils. They would visualize worst-case scenarios before they happened. This wasn't pessimism; it was preparation. By imagining what could go wrong, they were better able to handle it when it did.

Seneca wrote: "He robs present ills of their power who has perceived their coming beforehand." By thinking through negative outcomes in advance, we reduce their emotional impact and can plan contingencies.

## Charlie Munger's Application

Munger applies inversion constantly in his investment decisions at Berkshire Hathaway. He has said:

"It is remarkable how much long-term advantage people like us have gotten by trying to be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent."

This approach has guided Berkshire's investment strategy: don't try to pick winners, just avoid obvious losers. Don't try to time the market perfectly, just avoid buying at absurd valuations. Don't try to predict the future, just understand what businesses will likely still be around in 20 years.

The results speak for themselves. Berkshire Hathaway has compounded capital at roughly 20% annually for over 50 years — not by being brilliant, but by consistently avoiding stupidity.

## When Forward Thinking Fails

Some problems are so complex that forward thinking is insufficient. Consider:

- **Ethical dilemmas:** What is the right action? Sometimes easier to identify what's clearly wrong.
- **Complex systems:** Too many variables to predict outcomes. Easier to identify what will definitely fail.
- **Unknown unknowns:** We don't know what we don't know. But we often know what definitely won't work.

In these cases, inversion provides clarity where forward thinking produces confusion.

## The Mathematics of Inversion

Inversion is mathematically powerful because it transforms problems. If a problem is hard to solve in one direction, it may be easier in the opposite direction.

Consider the classic river-crossing puzzle: A farmer needs to transport a fox, a chicken, and grain across a river. The boat can only hold the farmer and one item. If left alone, the fox eats the chicken, and the chicken eats the grain. How does the farmer get everything across?

Forward thinking is messy. Inversion helps: What moves would guarantee failure? Leaving fox and chicken alone. Leaving chicken and grain alone. Avoid these, and the solution emerges.

## Building Inversion into Your Decision-Making

To make inversion a habit:

1. **Start with your goal.** What are you trying to achieve?

2. **Invert it.** What would guarantee failure?

3. **List the failure modes.** Be specific. "Hire wrong people" becomes "hire based on gut feeling without checking references."

4. **Create avoid-list.** Turn failure modes into actionable "don't do" items.

5. **Review regularly.** Check your decisions against the avoid-list.

This simple process, applied consistently, produces remarkable results over time.

## Conclusion

Inversion is a simple but powerful mental model. It doesn't require extraordinary intelligence — just the discipline to consider problems from both directions. By spending less time trying to be brilliant and more time avoiding obvious mistakes, you give yourself an advantage most people never consider.

As Charlie Munger might say: "All I want to know is where I'm going to fail, so I'll never go there."
