# Why people fail at learning languages

**Author:** Colin Gorrie — linguist, ancient language teacher (Latin, Old English), 20+ years language learning experience (French, German, Spanish, Latin, Sanskrit, Russian, Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Korean, Mandarin, Classical Chinese, Yiddish, Old English, Ancient Greek, Biblical Hebrew, Old Norse)
**Source:** The Dead Language Society (Substack)
**Published:** 2026-01-03

---

## Core Thesis

Most people fail at language learning because society has a wrong idea about **what language actually is**. Language is not a collection of facts to memorize or a skill to practice — it's an **abstract, unconscious, tacit knowledge**.

> "Language is an illusion — the illusion that particular sequences of sound waves, or ink on a page, have meaning. The goal is to acquire so much tacit knowledge that the illusion becomes so strong you can't encounter these sounds without associating them with a meaning."

Analogy: The Kanizsa triangle optical illusion — we see a triangle that isn't there because of unconscious knowledge about how shapes work. Language is the same: meaning is constructed unconsciously.

**Mental representation of the language** = the unconscious knowledge we're trying to acquire. Productive ability (speaking, writing) always lags behind comprehension — this is normal even in your native language.

> "I am a native English speaker. I can read Moby Dick. But I doubt I could write anything as good."

Knowledge of language is unconscious. Native speakers can tell you a sentence "just doesn't sound right" but not why. Example: "What did he make?" vs the impossible "What did he make and eggs?"

---

## The Core Activity: Decoding Messages

> "The core activity that builds up your mental representation of the language is **decoding messages in the language**."

**Counts as decoding:** Reading books, watching TV/movies, listening to podcasts, reading road signs, conversation (listening part).

**Does NOT count:** Reading textbooks, grammar exercises, studying flashcards, keeping app streaks, grammar-oriented classes.

These aren't useless but are **mostly useless** for building mental representation. They're tiring and unpleasant — leading to dropout.

> "To spend hours upon hours doing something hard without very much to show for it is a terribly dispiriting thing."

---

## Principle 1: Time-on-Task

The more contact hours (= time spent decoding messages), the faster progress.

**Efficiency varies by activity:**
- Reading/listening to podcasts → ~100% contact time
- Language apps (Duolingo-type) → ~50% (isolated sentences, flashcard activities)
- Some grammar classes → as low as 5% (10 hours → 0.5 contact hour)

**Time needed (Colin's experience):**
- Beginner level: **5 contact hours/month** = 10 min/day = steady progress
- Intermediate+: **10 hours/month** needed to maintain progress pace
- **20 hours/month** = dramatic improvements

> "It's easy to get discouraged, saying 'I've been learning Spanish for three months.' But if it's only five contact hours spread over three months doing the wrong things, lack of progress is exactly what you should expect."

---

## Principle 2: Quality — Comprehensible Input

Your input should be at a **95–99% comprehension level** (Goldilocks sweet spot). You should understand the message without understanding every word. Your mind works behind the scenes to build mental representation.

**4 comprehension-level experiments** (modified English passages):
- **99%:** 1 word blanked → easily guessable
- **95%:** Some blanks → still followable
- **90%:** Many blanks → severely impaired understanding
- **75%:** Massive blanks → near-gibberish

> "As much as possible, you want your experience to feel like the 99% and 95% passages, not the 90% or 75%."

**How to start:**
- Nothing aimed at native speakers will be comprehensible initially
- Seek **graded readers** — books written with limited vocabulary
- Live humans will adjust their language if patient enough (or paid)
- Early-stage motivation lets you power through suboptimal material temporarily
- After a few hundred words, previously hard material becomes just right

---

## Principle 3: Ownership

Consuming input results in excellent comprehension but a frustrating lag in production. The fix: activities that give **conscious "ownership"** of what you've been acquiring unconsciously.

> "The very activities that I said were not effective at building up your mental representation."

**Ownership activities:**
- Creating flash cards
- Explicit grammar learning (reading grammar explanations)
- Composition exercises

**What they do:** Make you feel comfortable as a **participant**, not a spectator, in the linguistic community.

**The multivitamin analogy:**
- Consuming input = your main course
- Ownership activities = multivitamins
- Vitamins are great supplements, but if you're eating nothing but vitamins, you're starving
- Most novice learners eat nothing but multivitamins and wonder why they're starving

> "They would do much better starting off with a programme of listening to and reading easy material for six months, and only then progressing to apps, classes, and grammar books."

---

## The 3 Principles Summary

1. **Time on task** — The more of the right things you do, the faster progress.
2. **Quality** — Consume input at 95–99% comprehension level.
3. **Ownership** — Supplement input with explicit study of vocab/grammar when you feel frustrated you can't produce things you already understand.

> "It is possible for you — for basically anyone — to learn another language, with the right tools and understanding. And it's one of the most rewarding experiences you can have."
