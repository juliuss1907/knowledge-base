# Why You Never Start (It's Not About Motivation)

There is a version of your life that you think about all the time. You can see it clearly. The business, the body, the creative project, the move abroad, whatever it is. And yet, every day you wake up and do nothing about it. This is really common, and it happens not because you're lazy. Please stop calling yourself that. I used to call myself a lazy person so much in the past, and now I know I did that because I didn't really understand the real explanation for why I was behaving that way.

So, let's talk about why you have all of these ambitious goals and dreams, but never start. And most importantly, what is going on in your brain when this happens. I'm Veronica, and on this channel I talk about nervous system regulation, building better habits, and learning how to actually live intentionally instead of just consuming content about it.

## It's Not a Motivation Problem — It's Your Nervous System

There's this research book by two amazing scientists who literally dedicated their careers to studying procrastination. They are Timothy Pychyl and Fuschia Sirois. So, when you think procrastination research, you should think about these two names. And in chapter eight of this research book, they lay out the idea that procrastination is not a time management problem. It's an emotion regulation problem.

So, again, the reason why you're not starting is not because you're bad at planning. It's because starting usually feels emotionally uncomfortable. It creates some sort of friction, and your brain is choosing to avoid that discomfort. Obviously, when you think about your big dreams and your goals, there are a lot of pleasant emotions that you're feeling. But, the moment you actually start thinking about, "Okay, I need to start working towards my dream, so I need to start doing something." And that usually makes you feel anxious, overwhelmed. And the thing is, your phone is right there. Social media is right there for you to enjoy. And instead of pushing through all of those uncomfortable feelings, your brain chooses the easiest emotional regulation method, which is being on your phone, not doing anything, and just doom scrolling. Your brain basically says, "Let me feel better right now." And scrolling is the fastest repair mood tool that you have. And that is not weakness. It is just how we all work.

Timothy Pychyl and Fuschia Sirois said that this doesn't mean that you don't know how to regulate your emotions. This means misregulation. So, there is a difference between these two words, under regulation and misregulation. And misregulation means that you are regulating your emotions. When you do that, when you scroll instead of working towards your goals, it just means that you're regulating your emotions that long term, it doesn't really help you, right? Because it makes things worse. Instead of actually working towards your goals and achieving your dreams, you're not starting, you're not doing anything. You avoid the task, you procrastinate, you feel better for 5 minutes, and then what happens? Guilt hits. And all of that guilt makes you feel even worse than the original discomfort, the original friction you were feeling when you were thinking about starting and working on your project. And then, what do we do to cope with guilt? We avoid again. We're trying to regulate our emotions, but what happens is misregulation. It creates this downward spiral.

The last piece of research that I think is really important is that Timothy Pychyl and Fuschia Sirois found that people who procrastinate the most have a weaker connection to their future self. When you put off working on your dream, your present self is stealing from your future self because it is still something that you want to do, it's still something that you have to do, but you're not doing it right now, so you will have to do it in the future, especially if you have a deadline. You basically dump all of that stress and panic on the future version of you, and that future version of you will have to deal with all of that later. And because your future self feels abstract, almost like a stranger, it's very easy to do it. Because if you think about dumping all of that stress on your close friend right now, you're probably not going to want to do it. But for some reason, most of us do it so easily when it comes to our future selves because we don't really think about it this way.

## The Window of Tolerance

Here's where your nervous system comes into this equation. There is a concept called the window of tolerance, and it was developed by a psychiatrist Dan Siegel. Basically, you have this zone, your window, where you can think clearly and make really rational decisions. When you're inside of this window, productivity advice actually works. Motivation works. Discipline works. Everything is just perfect. But when you're outside of that window, either here or here, you are overstimulated, overwhelmed, and you don't really know how to start. So your nervous system goes into survival mode, fight, flight, or freeze. And for most people who dream big but never start, you are in freeze, right here, which usually looks like this. You know what you need to get done, but for some reason you just feel unable to start, and so you prefer to scroll instead. That's not laziness, that's your nervous system saying, I don't feel safe enough to try. And when something like this happens, when your nervous system is in shutdown mode, we can't just rely on motivational quotes and just do it energy. We first have to heal our nervous system and work with that, making sure that we get into this window more often, instead of just being here or here.

## The Gym Analogy: Why We Resist Things We Enjoy

Think about exercise for a second. When you're lying on the couch, the thought of going to the gym to exercise is probably the last thing that you want to do right now. Like your entire body is saying no, I don't want to do it. You'd rather do literally anything else. But then you force yourself to go and then 20 minutes into the workout, you're like, I think I'm actually loving it. Like I actually like going to the gym and I'm so proud of myself. I'm so happy that I decided to go. And by the time you're done, you feel amazing, energized, clear-headed. And then you always think about this one thing. Why do I resist it so much at the very beginning?

The exact same thing is happening to your dreams and your goals. It's not like you don't want to achieve your goal. You do. You know you'll feel incredible once you start working on it and once you actually achieve it. But your brain treats starting as a threat. And this is a really well-documented thing in neuroscience.

Your amygdala is like your brain's threat detector. It runs a quick scan every time you're about to do something. And if that thing feels uncertain, uncomfortable, or emotionally loaded, your amygdala sends a signal that basically says danger, avoid, I don't want to do it, please no. And the prefrontal cortex is the front part of your brain. It's basically the part that does all the adult stuff. It typically fully develops and matures around age 25. The prefrontal cortex is responsible for planning, decision-making, thinking long-term, weighing consequences, and staying focused. It's the part of your brain that says, "Okay, I'm going to start working on my project, on my business today. I'm actually doing it." And when you want to start doing something, you feel all of that friction at the very beginning, and usually the amygdala takes over. Because the amygdala doesn't care about your five-year plan. It only cares about feeling better right now, this very second.

The wild thing is, research shows that it only takes about four to six repetitions of doing the same thing to actually recalibrate your nervous system, and start thinking about it in a different way, maybe reduce all of that friction. But the thing is, most of us never get past the first repetition, and that's why we never start, and we never continue.

## Veronica's Personal Example: Gym → Yoga

For many, many years, I didn't really have a consistent working out routine. Like, I knew exercising was important for me, was good for my health, for my body, but I always felt that friction. Like, every single time I would go to the gym, I felt horrible. Like, a lot of the negative emotions, and all I thought about was that I didn't want to do it. So, my amygdala would take over, and I would skip a lot of my workout sessions. I felt dread, to be honest, and no matter how many times the rational part of my brain, the prefrontal cortex, would tell me, "No, Veronica, it's okay. Just go. You're going to feel better later." My amygdala would always win, and so I wouldn't really continue, and I was just very inconsistent.

So, one day I was like, "Okay, I'm not going to go to the gym. I'm going to work on it in a different way." I started thinking about all of the forms of movement that I enjoyed. Not like going to the gym and lifting weights, but all of the different ways you can move your body, you can exercise, you can work out. And so, the first thing that I wanted to try, that was on my list, was rock climbing and bouldering. And so, I actually did that for like a year or two, and I really enjoyed it. It was something that helped me bring the consistency back into my working out routine. And then I just went down my list, and I tried every single activity. Things like ballet, yoga, Pilates. And what I was doing without realizing is something scientists call cognitive change. I wasn't forcing myself to push through the dread. I was changing how the task was framed in my brain. Instead of go to the gym, which my amygdala had flagged as something absolutely awful, I turned it into explore all the ways to move your body. And my alarm system had no reason to resist that. The goal was never to go to the gym and lift weights. The goal was to move my body because at that point any form of movement was better for me than just doing nothing and being sedentary all day. And one thing that stuck was obviously yoga. I really fell in love with it and I even became a yoga teacher at some point. All because I stopped fighting my amygdala and started working with it.

You don't always have to bulldoze through all of that resistance. Sometimes you can find a door that's already slightly open.

## Consumption Is Not Progress

When you watch a self-improvement video about your dream, when you read the book, save the post, plan the plan, your brain gives you a little dopamine hit because learning about your dream activates a very similar reward response as actually going and working on it. It feels like progress. You feel productive. You feel like you're actually doing something to get closer to your goal. But you're not. You're consuming and consumption is not doing. And this is actually a form of avoidance. It is something that so many of us struggle with. Your brain has figured out that it can get all of the good emotions of working towards my dream without any emotional discomfort of actually starting. So it keeps choosing that path. Why risk failure if you can feel productive by watching just another video?

I lived through that exact experience with YouTube because I wanted to start my own YouTube channel ever since I was a teenager. And in 2019, I was 19 at that time, I actually recorded my very first YouTube video. I edited it, but I never posted it. I just couldn't press publish. And the reason wasn't that I didn't want it. Like it wasn't my dream anymore. It still was. I wanted it really badly. The reason was once I sat down and I actually did the work, I scripted it, I planned everything, I sat down to record the video, I then worked on the editing, I realized how hard it was. Like how much effort every single video would take. And my brain immediately went to if this is what it takes to make just one video, how am I going to go through all of this every single week? So, I quit. I froze and I waited another full year.

Only at the end of 2020, I was like, "Okay, I'm actually going to do it. I'm going to start and I'm going to continue." And even then, I think for me it was terrifying because the entry point I created for myself was extremely high. I was watching all of those different creators with years of experience on YouTube and I was comparing myself to them. And plus, I'm not a native English speaker, so I also had all of that pressure of making sure that my English was perfect when I was recording a YouTube video. I was comparing my non-existent video to someone else's 500th video. And obviously, you know, this discrepancy is so high, the pressure, the friction that we're experiencing is so high, and our amygdala is just like, "Nope. Like you're not going to do it. I want to feel nice and comfortable right now, so I'm not going to do it. That's way too much pressure."

Comparison really kills dreams before they even begin. We see someone online doing the thing that we want to do, and instead of feeling inspired and motivated and thinking that, "Wow, I can do this, too." We just feel all of this dread and fear, and we think that we will never be able to do anything like this. And so we don't start because we think that our starting line should be the same as someone else's finishing line.

## What You Can Actually Do

### 1. Stop Pushing Through

I know it probably sounds counterintuitive, but stop pushing through. That's my example with the gym because I was forcing myself so much, and I was never consistent with it. The moment I started working with my nervous system instead of against it, everything changed for me. I was trying to look for a version of moving my body, of exercising, that my nervous system was okay with. I was trying to find that door that was already open. Because your brain can't plan when it's in survival mode. So before you try to be productive, try to be regulated. Try to work on that first. You need to feel safe in your body before your brain will let you take a risk. And starting on your dream feels like a risk to your dysregulated nervous system.

### 2. Be Honest: Are You Doing or Just Consuming?

I say this as someone who recorded an entire video in 2019, and I just deleted it, and I spent an entire year watching other creators instead of being one. I was consuming. I felt like I was getting closer to my dream, but in reality, I was getting more and more comfortable not doing it.

### 3. The Low Entry Principle

If you create extremely high expectations, and you want everything to be perfect immediately, it is going to be impossible for you to start. Like this morning, something happened to my hair. It was not a good hair day for me, and my bangs just look very flat. And so, I could have said, "Okay, I'm not going to do anything today. I'm not going to record a video. It doesn't really matter, because everything has to be perfect before I can even begin." But the thing is, you guys don't even know all of those emotions and struggles and things that I'm going through every single day. Like, if I hadn't mentioned that today I had a bad hair day, most of you would probably never guess. And that's the thing, we feel all of that friction, and we choose to regulate ourselves in a different way, a way that is pushing us further and further from our dream. Because I could have said, "Okay, today I'm not recording anything," and that would have dumped all of that responsibility on my future self. Maybe tomorrow or the day after tomorrow, I will still be recording. So, why not make it today?

It's fine. It's a bad hair day. It happens. It happens to everyone, and there's nothing I can do about it. The sooner you learn that and the sooner you make peace with it and learn how to regulate your emotions in those situations, so that you're still getting closer towards your dream, not running away from it, the better you will actually feel.
