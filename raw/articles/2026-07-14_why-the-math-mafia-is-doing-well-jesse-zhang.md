# Why the "Math Mafia" is doing well

**Author:** Jesse Zhang (@thejessezhang)
**Source:** X (Twitter Article)
**Published:** 2026-07-13

---

## The Hudson River Trading Intern Class That Went Viral

A few months ago, the Hudson River Trading intern class went viral because the group included:
- Jeff Yan (founder of Hyperliquid)
- Scott Wu (cofounder of Cognition)
- Alexandr Wang (founder of Scale)
- Jesse Zhang (@DecagonAI)

This sparked a broader conversation about how the generation who did math, coding, and science olympiads growing up represents a disproportionate amount of today's founders.

There's more of them: Johnny Ho (cofounder of Perplexity), Steven Hao and Walden Yan (other cofounders of Cognition), Demi Guo (cofounder of Pika), Albert Gu (cofounder of Cartesia), and many more.

## What is an Olympiad?

Academic competitions that are difficult and timed. For math, it's typically 6 hard problems over two days, 4.5 hours per day.

**Example problems:**
- "Determine all triples of positive integers (a, b, c) such that each of the numbers ab − c, bc − a, ca − b is a power of 2."
- "Suppose a, b, c are the side lengths of a triangle. Prove that a²b(a−b) + b²c(b−c) + c²a(c−a) ≥ 0, and determine when equality occurs."

Everyone is ranked nationally and then eventually internationally. The top students get brought to a summer camp, which is where you first get to know each other.

## Why Olympiad Background Translates to Founding Companies

**1. Problem-solving traits carry over**
Many challenges founders face are difficult and ambiguous — not just technical challenges, but how to build an org, how to design a GTM strategy. Being able to slowly chew through them systematically and rationally is a big advantage.

**2. High-pressure competitive environments develop resilience**
Doing well requires extreme grinding and sacrifice over many years. Doing well really makes you believe you can do hard things. This breeds people who normalize hard work and don't shy away from intense competition.

Some knew every olympiad problem by heart from the last 30 years. It's hard not to be inspired by that.

**3. Community effect**
Having a community of friends doing the same thing is very powerful. When the author was in high school, he saw the first couple of folks start companies. Being able to learn from peers doing the same thing lets you draw inspiration from successes and avoid mistakes.

The group in the photo was the first-ever HRT intern class. Shortly after that, Alexandr dropped out to start Scale — an inspiring journey to see up close.

**4. Quant finance as real-world math contests**
Quant firms (Hudson River Trading, Citadel, Jane Street, D.E. Shaw) are basically real-world forms of math contests where work ethic and problem-solving are directly rewarded. Citadel kicked off their internship with a literal live math contest for all the interns.

## The "Paypal Mafia" Parallel

Like how the "Paypal Mafia" produced such a dense group of people starting companies, investment firms, and funds, the author suspects we'll see more and more people from the Olympiad community in the coming years.

The Research Science Institute (a high school research camp) alumni group includes Michael Truell (Cursor) and Karim Atiyeh (Ramp). The author has four other alumni at Decagon.

## Key Takeaway

The author would heavily encourage olympiads or academic contests for all kids, depending on what you're interested in. The community will live with you for your whole life.

## Caveat

Founding a company requires many more skills than what you learn in olympiads. Olympiads are generally individual contests, while startups are very much a team sport. Contests are great for solving problems you receive but often in life, you need to come up with your own problems.
