## The Leverage Thesis

Leverage is the name of the game. If you're a long-term reader of this publication, you already know our love for leverage. And this time, we will be focusing on asymmetric advantages.

Most people assume that effort = results. A linear assumption: "Just grind harder to get better results." And it's true to some degree. Obviously, a writer who writes for 2 hours instead of 1 hour will get more work done.

But that's beside the point. Because effort is a strict volume game that everyone can play. The issue is that most people don't even know the following:

1. Which game they're in
2. What position they currently hold
3. What information they possess that others lack

Effort is a multiplier to those aspects.

The reason a writer improves his payoff by writing more is that he knows that publishing strengthens his position and improves his writing ability (which is encoded in the form of information). His effort compounds into better positioning and superior information.

Now take a construction worker as a negative example of this. Working more means that he gets more done, but it worsens his position over time because his rest is essential for tackling the work of the coming days. His effort compounds negatively by accumulating fatigue.

These are two different extremes, and it's not about blue-collar being bad and white-collar being superior; it should merely reinforce the argument that effort is not always a viable solution, and it's about whether your effort compounds a renewable asset.

The writer's hours establish something that's still there tomorrow. The construction worker's hours cost him tomorrow to pay for today.

Now that we've settled this, let's get into the actual strategies.

## Axiom 1: Choose the Game Before You Try to Win It

The instinct to get better at what you're doing is not guaranteed to improve your situation. The environment you play in is the problem.

Every time you partake in a game, you enter its structure. In 99% of cases, this structure is already predefined simply due to the existence of other players.

So if you're not actively trying to reinvent the wheel or create a new language, you're playing inside a system that isn't concerned with your preferences, because you are not (yet) influential enough to impact its structure.

### Most competitive games have a structure that resembles this:

**The most influential players**, or the founders, are at the top of the hierarchy. They have surpassed the game itself and are so influential that they can shift the behaviors of the players by themselves or adjust the structure. Think of how billionaires can impact the stock market or politicians creating laws that citizens must adhere to.

**The payoff matrix of the game.** Whenever influential actors aren't actively meddling with the structure of a game, it alone dictates the behaviors of the other players. Every game worth being in is a system that can largely sustain itself.

**The players**, which can roughly be categorized by skill and information: the advanced, who maximize their payoff from a well-built position while possessing vital information about the game's structure; the intermediate, who show promising progress but still have a long way to go; and the beginners, where everyone starts, with the strongest information asymmetry and no positioning.

Notice what differentiates the beginners from the intermediate and the advanced. It is clearly not their effort. It's their relative level of information and their position. An advanced player can allow themselves to be lazy because they have a solid supporting structure.

The beginner cannot compete with this, regardless of their level of excellence. It's like being surrounded by strings and not knowing which one to pull, so you're forced to go through every single string in hopes of finding the right one. That's precisely what lack of information does to you.

### Gathering Intel

Before you enter, familiarize yourself with the game's environment. Look out for advanced and intermediate players, and analyze how they play the game. This is how you alleviate your initial lack of information.

More importantly, take a glance at the game itself. What is the most achievable outcome? If the ceiling of the outcome is low, then you're usually not in a game that compounds your effort.

Look at the pieces on the board, analyze the figures, their position, and what they intend to do to maximize their payoff. Don't reinvent the wheel; figure out what works and use that, then add effort to it.

## Axiom 2: Asymmetry Devours Intensity

The whole reason "being lazy" is not immediately a losing strategy is that working hard won't average out your results in your favor.

This effort grindset assumes symmetrical games. It applies a rigid logic to work: "Either work harder or suffer the setbacks." But work is not a linear graph. Rarely, if ever, is your effort proportional to your results.

The most common delusion that leads people to continuously chase symmetric positions is that they believe every competitor is an equal. A noble idea, yet flawed nonetheless, because now you treat yourself and others like replicas of each other.

Think about it: if you assume that everyone can work hard to become rich, influential, or powerful, then why would you waste your time with building something entirely replaceable? You end up going from one rat race to the next.

Prioritizing asymmetric positions is the polar opposite. They frequently require some investment to reach them, but compound upward. What makes them the lazier option is that they do not need a high hit rate. You set them up, and they turn into leverage.

This weaves into a solid rule of thumb:

- **Asymmetric positions** are positions that work for you. They are leverage.
- **Symmetric positions** are positions in which you work for them. They are a liability.

Think of a company trying to advertise a new product. They could limit themselves to their own channels, and work harder to get in front of more potential customers. That is a feasible possibility. But it's difficult. It demands immense effort and commitment over a long time.

That's why most companies settle for the alternative: they pay for ads. They trade money for positioning. That's the investment. Over its lifespan, the advertised product is likely to grant them a profit that far outweighs their cost spent, while saving time.

### The Payoff Check

It doesn't always have to be capital that converts your symmetric position into asymmetric leverage. Money, like many other factors, is a screwdriver. It fits one occasion, while being a mismatch to another.

Your imperative is to identify the screws, then methodically select the right screwdriver.

As such, you must examine the payoff. In most cases, there is a hidden amplifier in any game you play. Ask, "What multiplier can get me to my desired payoff?"

Sometimes it's money. Other times it's contacts. Every now and then, the solution may even be to build something long-lasting, like a social media presence.

What you must understand is that the asymmetric position requires an upfront sacrifice for an easier time afterward.

## Axiom 3: Information Is the Real Game

Information. The cheapest resource that changes everything. "Work smarter, not harder," you've heard it before. Even though it's frowned upon in hustle culture, it is nonetheless a very significant advantage.

In comparison to effort, information is not expendable. You either have it, or you don't.

The calculation for knowledge is particularly interesting because it is the rare resource where price doesn't scale with stakes at all. An hour of research costs the same whether it saves you a weekend or three years.

Information is so incredibly valuable because it can eliminate the uninformed while crowning the insightful.

### But what is information?

Most assume it's some kind of recipe for success, some kind of knowledgeable life hack that alters your overall trajectory.

It doesn't even have to be this drastic to enable success.

Sometimes, good information is centered around knowing the risks, the pitfalls, and what to avoid rather than what to do. The logic is simple: those who can avoid wasting time will advance to their desired destination far more expeditiously.

### Reverse-Engineer Success

The overwhelming majority of people lack information because they ignore it. Most advice can be found by yourself, if you care to look for it.

Find someone who succeeded in a similar or the same game. Ask them if possible. If that's not an option, you must resort to research and careful study of their moves.

Yes, most people already pretend they do just that. I've genuinely heard the same thing a million times by now, something similar to:

"Oh yeah, I studied the greatest people in existence, like Jeff Bezos and Steve Jobs and those guys."

Now be honest with yourself. Did you study them, or did you just read a case study from someone else? The latter is only acceptable as information if you use it as your launchpad, as a map so that you know where to seek insight.

Then, it's time to go to the drawing board and write down the steps they took.
