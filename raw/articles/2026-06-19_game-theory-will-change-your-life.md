let's play a game.

you and your partner in crime just got arrested. police separated you into different cells. you can't communicate or coordinate.

detective walks into your cell with an offer.

"here's the deal. betray your partner, testify against them, and you walk free while they rot for 10 years. but if they betray you first, you're the one getting 10 years while they walk."

he leans back in his chair. lets that sink in.

"or, you both stay silent. we can only hold you for a year each. minor charges."

you're sitting there trying to think through every possibility.

if you both stay silent: 1 year each. best collective outcome.
if you betray and they stay silent: you're free, they get 10 years.
if they betray and you stay silent: they're free, you get 10 years.
if you both betray: 5 years each.

the logical move for the group is silence. 1 year is nothing. walk out, move on with life.

but from your individual perspective, betrayal seems safer.

because what if your partner is sitting in their cell right now thinking the exact same thing? what if they're already cutting a deal?

if they betray you and you stayed silent, you're fucked. 10 years.

but if you betray them first, worst case you both get 5 years. best case you walk free.

so you betray. and your partner betrays. and you both get 5 years.

worse outcome than if you'd just trusted each other and stayed silent.

this is the prisoner's dilemma. and you just lived it.

welcome to game theory.

## What Is Game Theory?

game theory is the scientific study of strategic decision-making.

it analyzes situations where your outcome depends not just on what you do, but on what others do.

and that's basically every human interaction.

your salary negotiation? game theory. your boss wants to pay you less, you want to earn more. so the outcome depends on how you both play.

your relationship? game theory. you have preferences, your partner has preferences. sometimes they align, sometimes they conflict. how you navigate that determines the relationship outcome.

choosing a restaurant with friends? game theory. everyone has preferences but needs to coordinate. someone has to compromise or nothing happens.

applying for jobs? game theory. you're competing against other candidates. your strategy affects their chances, their strategy affects yours.

posting on social media? game theory. you're competing for attention in a finite attention economy. your content strategy affects everyone else's reach.

all of it. all human interaction. strategic decision-making where outcomes depend on multiple players' choices.

and most people are terrible at it because they don't understand the structure of the games they're playing.

they optimize for the wrong things
cooperate when they should defect. defect when they should cooperate.
stay in bad equilibria because they don't see the better outcomes available.

game theory makes the invisible visible.

## Nash Equilibrium: Stuck in Suboptimal Outcomes

john nash figured out something that changed how we understand strategic behavior.

in any game with multiple players, there's often a point where nobody wants to change their strategy even though everyone could be better off if they all changed together.

this is nash equilibrium. stable but not necessarily optimal.

**real life example:**

you and your partner have been living in the same city for five years. you both have decent jobs and a decent life but you've both outgrown it.

you want to move to a bigger city. career opportunities. higher salary. professional growth.

your partner wants to move somewhere quieter. space. peace. better for raising kids eventually.

you both want to move but in opposite directions.

so you talk about it. neither wants to give up their dream for the other's.

moving to the big city for your career feels like asking your partner to sacrifice their dream.

moving to the quiet town for their peace feels like you're giving up your ambitions.

so you do nothing. stay where you are. it's the safest bet because nobody has to sacrifice.

years pass.

you watch dream job opportunities in bigger markets come and go. promotions you would've gotten and salary jumps you missed.

your partner never gets the space and peace they craved. still living in the medium-sized city that's neither career hub nor peaceful retreat.

both stuck. both worse off than if one of you had just made a move.

this is nash equilibrium. you're both making the best choice given what the other is doing. but you're locked into a shit outcome because changing would require coordination you can't achieve.

**and this pattern is everywhere.**

companies stuck in pricing wars. both would profit more if they raised prices together but neither wants to be the first because the other could undercut them. so they stay in low-profit equilibrium.

friends stuck in shallow relationships. everyone wants deeper connection but nobody wants to be vulnerable first so you all stay surface-level. stable but unsatisfying. no one wants to share the uncomfortable story or ask the deep question.

political parties stuck in polarization. both would benefit from cooperation but defecting is safer so you get permanent gridlock.

the key to escaping nash equilibrium is communication and commitment. you need to coordinate strategy changes simultaneously.

but most people never even recognize they're stuck in an equilibrium. they think this is just how things are.

it's not. it's a strategic trap you could escape if you understood the game structure.

> Cooperation for mutual benefit - a survival strategy very common in nature - is one that humanity needs to emulate - Eugene Odum

## The Ultimatum Game: When Fairness Destroys Value

you and two friends are trying to book a private table at the best restaurant in town for a festival weekend.

900 dollars total. split evenly it would be about 300 each. everyone's excited.

but before you pay the deposit, one friend - let's call him 'the proposer' - changes the deal.

"i'll only go if i pay 100," he says. "i'm not drinking and i won't eat much so you two can split the rest."

you and the other friend look at each other. that's 400 each for you instead of 300.

"fuck that," you say. "we agreed on even split."

proposer refuses to budge. "100 or i'm out."

you and the other friend refuse to cover his share. principle matters.

proposer holds firm.

restaurant won't hold the table without full payment. booking falls through.

the night you've all been looking forward to is gone.

this is the ultimatum game. someone proposes a split. you accept or reject. if you reject, nobody gets anything.

the "rational" move would've been accepting the 400 split. you still get the experience. still get the night out. just costs more.

but humans aren't purely rational. fairness matters. pride matters. being taken advantage of feels worse than losing the opportunity.

this is why deals fall apart even when both parties would benefit. why negotiations fail. why partnerships dissolve. why simple agreements turn to shit.

someone makes an offer that feels unfair so the other person rejects it out of principle. but in the end both end up with nothing instead of something.

happens in business constantly. startup founder and investor can't agree on valuation so the deal dies. company never gets funded. both lose.

happens in relationships. one person wants something they think is reasonable. other person sees it as unfair demand. both dig in. relationship ends.

happens in workplace negotiations. employee asks for raise they think they deserve. boss offers less than they think is fair. employee quits. company loses good employee, employee loses job.

the ultimatum game shows how fast good opportunities vanish when fairness and pride collide.

sometimes protecting the principle is worth more than the payoff.

the key is recognizing which game you're really playing before the clock runs out.

are you playing for the optimal outcome or are you playing to punish unfairness?

both are valid. but you need to know which one you've chosen.

## Zero-Sum vs Positive-Sum Games

**zero-sum game:** your gain is my loss.

poker. sports. promotions with one opening.

shit like that is a fixed pie. whatever you get, i lose. whatever i get, you lose.

in poker one player ends up with all the chips. in sports one team ends up winning the championship.

**positive-sum game:** we can both win. business partnerships. trade. relationships.

the pie can grow. your success doesn't require my failure.

**and here's where most people fuck up…**

they treat positive-sum games like zero-sum games.

they compete when they should cooperate. they hoard when they should share. they optimize for relative position instead of absolute gain. they become selfish when they should share.

**i'll give a classic example that many of you can probably relate to: workplace dynamics.**

you and a coworker are both competent. you could collaborate, share knowledge, make each other better because the team succeeds and you both get raises.

positive-sum game. pie grows for everyone.

but you're worried they'll look better than you. so you withhold information. protect your knowledge. maybe even subtly sabotage them.

zero-sum thinking. you're competing for relative position.

result: team performs worse. both of you look worse. raises are smaller or nonexistent.

you turned a positive-sum game into a zero-sum game through competitive mindset.

happens in relationships constantly. partners viewing their needs as competing instead of complementary.

"if you get what you want, i don't get what i want."

but often both people's needs could be met with creative solutions. the relationship pie could grow.

treating it like zero-sum creates unnecessary conflict because both people fight for their share of a fixed pie instead of growing the pie together.

**or business is another good example.** two companies in the same space viewing each other as pure competitors.

they could partner and share resources. expand the market together while both growing faster.

instead they undercut each other. race to bottom on pricing.

zero-sum thinking in a positive-sum environment.

the shift happens when you start asking: "is this actually a competition or could we both win?"

most games have more cooperative potential than people recognize. but competitive instinct kicks in automatically.

understanding whether you're in zero-sum or positive-sum changes everything about strategy.

## Repeated Games: Why Reputation Matters

**one-shot games:** you play once, never see the other player again. incentive to defect, screw them over, maximize short-term gain.

**repeated games:** you'll play with the same person many times. incentive to cooperate, build trust, optimize long-term.

**and this is why reputation matters.**

life isn't one-shot. you're playing repeated games constantly. with coworkers. with clients. with friends. with partners. with people in general.

screw someone over for short-term gain? word gets out and nobody trusts you.

congrats you fucked yourself and now you're locked out of future cooperation.

the prisoner's dilemma we started with? it changes completely in repeated play.

if you know you'll face the same prisoner situation multiple times with the same partner, cooperation becomes rational.

betray them once, they'll betray you forever. neither of you can trust anymore. locked into perpetual 5-year sentences.

but if you cooperate and they cooperate, you both get 1 year every time. way better long-term outcome.

this is why "tit for tat" is such an effective strategy in repeated games.

cooperate on first interaction. then mirror whatever they did. they cooperated? you cooperate. they defected? you defect next round.

simple strategy. incredibly robust. wins more repeated game tournaments than complex strategies.

and it maps perfectly to real life.

someone helps you? help them back.

someone screws you over? don't help them again.

builds reputation. people learn they can't exploit you but they also learn you're willing to cooperate with cooperators.

this is why freelancers obsess over reviews. why businesses care about brand reputation. why professionals maintain relationships. why people care about feedback.

in repeated games, your reputation is your most valuable asset.
