# The Reader Comment That Sent Me Down a Systems Thinking Rabbit Hole

Source: [PM Researcher](https://open.substack.com/pub/pmresearcher/p/the-reader-comment-that-sent-me-down)
Author: Nicole

---

A reader left a comment under one of my systems thinking articles that made me pause. She asked if I had seen any research on people who naturally think in systems, rather than learning systems thinking as a skill.

I loved the question because it caught something I had not fully addressed.

When I write about systems thinking, I usually treat it as a discipline. A way of seeing. A way of slowing down before we rush into analysis, timelines, deliverables, and solutions.

But her question made me wonder:
- What if some people already think this way?
- What if some people naturally see the relationships before the parts?

So I did what curious people do. I went down a rabbit hole.

## My first instinct was to say yes

Some people do seem naturally wired to think this way. They walk into a situation and immediately notice the relationships. They ask who is affected. They wonder what happens downstream. They are less interested in the isolated task and more interested in the pattern behind the task.

In a project environment, these are the people who ask questions like:
- What problem are we actually solving?
- Who is missing from this conversation?
- What happens if this decision works exactly as designed?
- What behavior are we accidentally incentivizing?
- Why does this same issue keep showing up in different forms?

That kind of thinking can look natural. And maybe part of it is. But the more I read, the more I started to see a distinction.

> **We may be born with the ingredients of systems thinking. But we are not born with the discipline of systems thinking.**

## We are born looking for causes

Humans are cause-seeking creatures. Even very young children are constantly testing the world. If I push this, what happens? If I drop this, who reacts? If I do this again, does the same thing happen?

That matters because systems thinking depends on causal reasoning. You cannot think in systems if you are not curious about cause, effect, and interaction.

One review on human causal learning describes causal understanding as a defining feature of human cognition. Children do not just observe the world; they learn by acting on it, intervening in it, and eventually developing more abstract ways to reason about what causes what.

So yes, there is something natural here.

But causal thinking is not the same as systems thinking. A person can identify a cause and still miss the system.

They can say, "The project is delayed because the team missed a milestone."

That may be true.

But a systems thinker asks:
- Why was that milestone vulnerable in the first place?
- Was ownership unclear?
- Were dependencies hidden?
- Did the reporting structure reward optimism until it was too late?
- Was the schedule built around hope instead of constraints?

That is a different level of thinking.

## The ability to transfer patterns

One study looked at children between ages 4 and 7. The researchers wanted to know whether children could recognize the same causal structure across different stories.

The interesting part was that children as young as five could understand the causal structure of an individual story. But the older children, around six and seven, were more likely to recognize the same structure across different stories.

That felt important.

Because systems thinking is not just noticing what happened once. It is recognizing a structure that keeps reproducing itself.

It is the ability to say:
- This is not just a staffing issue. This is a coordination issue.
- This is not just a communication problem. This is a governance problem.
- This is not just one delayed decision. This is a system that only escalates when delay becomes visible.

That ability to transfer a pattern from one situation to another is a major part of systems thinking. And it seems to develop.

## Some people may notice relationships sooner

Then I found myself reading about relational reasoning. Relational reasoning is basically the ability to think about relationships among multiple things. Not just the things themselves, but how they connect.

That sounds simple. It is what allows someone to compare two situations that look different on the surface but operate in similar ways underneath. Research on relational reasoning connects it to logical thinking, novel problem solving, and the ability to integrate multiple relationships at once (Wendelken et al.).

This is where I think the "natural systems thinker" idea has some truth. Some people may have a stronger instinct for relationships. They notice dependencies faster.

They hear one decision and immediately think about the second- and third-order effects.

They are bothered by simple explanations because they can feel the missing context.

They may not know the formal language of systems thinking yet. They may not talk about feedback loops, emergence, boundaries, or leverage points. But they are already sensing the shape of the system.

I have met people like this.

I also think many of them have spent years being told they are "overthinking."

## But intuition is not enough

This is where I had to check myself.

Because I do believe some people are naturally more relational or contextual in their thinking.

But I do not think that automatically makes someone a systems thinker. Systems thinking is more than sensing that everything is connected. Honestly, "everything is connected" can become a trap. If everything is connected, then where do you start?

- What boundary matters?
- Which relationship is most important?
- What feedback loop is driving the behavior?
- What can actually be changed?
- What is noise, and what is structure?

This is why systems thinking has to become a discipline. The instinct may be natural. The practice is learned.

## Holistic thinking is close, but not the same

Another trail I followed was the research on holistic versus analytical thinking.

A well-known paper describes holistic thinkers as paying more attention to context and relationships, while analytical thinkers focus more on objects, categories, and formal logic (Nisbett et al.).

That sounds very close to systems thinking.

But I do not think they are identical.

**Holistic thinking is a tendency.**  
**Systems thinking is a method.**

A holistic thinker might feel that a project delay is connected to culture, leadership, incentives, and communication.

A systems thinker tries to map how.

That difference matters.

Because in real work, especially project work, vague complexity is not enough. We still have to make decisions. We still have to define boundaries. We still have to choose where to intervene.

**Systems thinking does not replace analysis. It improves the quality of analysis.**

## Where I landed

So, are some people born systems thinkers?

My answer now is: **Not exactly.**

I think we are born with systems-thinking ingredients.

We are born curious about cause and effect. We are born able to learn from interaction. We are born with the capacity to notice patterns. Some people may also have a stronger natural tendency toward relational or contextual thinking.

But systems thinking itself has to be developed.

It has to be practiced.
It has to be named.
It has to be sharpened.

A natural systems thinker may see relationships quickly. But a disciplined systems thinker knows how to test those relationships, define the boundary, identify the feedback, and ask where change is actually possible.

**That is the part we have to learn.**

## Why this matters

If systems thinking is only something people are born with, then we treat it like a personality trait.

Some people have it. Some people do not.

But if systems thinking is a capability, then we can build it.

- We can teach teams to pause before they break work into tasks.
- We can ask better questions before building timelines.
- We can stop treating recurring problems as isolated incidents.
- We can look at the structure producing the behavior.

That is especially important in project environments, where the pressure to move quickly can make us analytical too soon.

We create the spreadsheet.  
We assign the owner.  
We build the dashboard.  
We update the timeline.

But if we have not understood the system, all that precision may simply help us manage the wrong problem more efficiently.

That is why I keep coming back to this idea:

> **Systems thinking must come before analytical thinking because analysis needs context.**

Without systems thinking, analysis can become premature precision.

With systems thinking, analysis becomes useful.

## The answer I would give the reader

If I were answering the comment now, I would say:

I did look into it, and the answer seems to be both yes and no. Yes, some people may naturally notice relationships, patterns, and context earlier than others. But no, I do not think we are born as fully formed systems thinkers. We are born with the raw material. Systems thinking is what happens when that raw material becomes a practice.

Some people may see the system sooner.  
But seeing the system clearly still takes work.

— Nicole

## Relevant research sources

1. **Goddu, M. K., & Gopnik, A.** — "The development of human causal learning and reasoning." This review explores how human causal reasoning develops from early action and intervention into abstract theories, counterfactual thinking, and future-oriented reasoning (PhilArchive).

2. **Rett, A., Amemiya, J., Hwang, B., Goldwater, M., & Walker, C. M.** — "Children's recognition of causal system categories across superficially distinct events." This study found that children's ability to use shared causal structure across different stories develops gradually between ages 4 and 7 (PubMed).

3. **Wendelken, C., et al.** — "Neurocognitive development of relational reasoning." This article examines relational reasoning, including children's development of relational integration and associated prefrontal brain activity (PMC).

4. **Nisbett, R. E., Peng, K., Choi, I., & Norenzayan, A.** — "Culture and systems of thought: holistic versus analytic cognition." This paper distinguishes holistic cognition, which attends to context and relationships, from analytical cognition, which emphasizes objects, categories, and formal logic (PubMed).
