# Thinking about Thinking

When i first heard the word metacognition in a cognitive psychology class years ago, i loved it instantly. thinking about thinking. we can actually watch our own minds work? the word itself felt cool—this was right before facebook became meta and the term got attached to everything.

To understand what it is, first, we should know the first-order cognition—the automatic thought process. when we live on autopilot, doing simple tasks without much thought, it just comes naturally and easily. we navigate everyday routines the way we always have; our brain saves energy by turning most tasks into habits. it's when we have an immediate emotional response, or execute an automatic action. essential for survival, but we can't rely on it for everything.

Metacognition is the second layer. the shift from being the experience to observing and controlling it. someone replies to you with one word and you feel a flare of irritation—first layer. noticing that you've been irritated since this morning and this message probably isn't the reason—second one.

If you speak more than one language you already know how it feels. picking the right word, adjusting for who's listening, catching the exact moment something doesn't translate the way you meant it. you're not just speaking, you're watching yourself speak.

In school i had a classmate who barely studied and did well, and a friend who studied for hours and did average every time, semester after semester. the first ones looked like talent or raw intelligence. it wasn't. she knew exactly where she was weak—she'd say things like i'm fine on the theory but i always mess up the dates—and she'd go memorize the dates. the second one read everything from the beginning, in order, for hours. she would wake up at 4am sometimes to study. no i understand that the first classmate who we called lazy but smart was actually using metacognition.

John flavell named this whole area in 1979, and it's split into two parts: knowing how your own mind works, and doing something with that knowledge. the second part is where my studious friend fell down. she had idea what were her blind spots and strengths, but she didnt act upon it and studied everything. while the other friend knew her weaknesses, strengths, when and where she could focus the most and used that knowledge strategically.

So knowing yourself isn't enough. acting on what you know is the part that counts.

And this isn't only about studying. using metacognition in daily life makes things easier and more productive—you waste less time on approaches that aren't working, you make fewer decisions you have to undo later, and you spend less energy on problems that were never there.

My avoidance is a good example of that last one. i decide, before starting, that something is going to be awful; an email i've been avoiding, a phone call, a conversation i know is coming. the dread builds for days and feels like correct information. then i do it, it takes eleven minutes, and it's fine.

What should happen next is that i learn from it. mostly i don't, because usually i never get to the eleven minutes. i put it off, so the prediction never gets tested. and from the inside, avoiding always looks like it worked, you avoided something, nothing bad happened, so avoiding must have been right.

## Cognitive Distortions

These patterns have a name. therapists call them cognitive distortions: habitual ways our thinking bends what's actually happening, usually in the same few directions. everyone uses them. they're just the automatic layer producing conclusions faster than anything can check them. these three are i see constantly.

**Black-and-white thinking.** everything sorts into two categories with nothing in between. one meeting goes badly, therefore i'm bad at my job. one lie from a loved one and he is immidiately bad person and vice versa. two boxes are easier to hold than a spectrum and complexity.

**Catastrophizing.** one small piece of evidence and the mind builds the whole disaster from it. a message goes unanswered for three hours, so they're angry, so something's wrong, maybe an accident happened. it takes about a second, and your world is ending.

**Mind reading.** deciding you know what someone thought of you from a pause or a tone. this is the one i did the most. and the few times i've actually checked, they were thinking about something else entirely.

What these have in common is speed. each one gives you a finished conclusion with no working shown. you can't argue with a thought when you are inside it, because from in there it doesn't look like a distortion. it looks like a fact. to argue you should move one layer up, distance yourself out. check it from outside.

## Practical Tools

- **Explain it out loud** to someone who can interrupt. reading something and nodding gives you the feeling of understanding it. explaining it to a person who can stop you and ask what you mean gives how much you actually understood it or a quick discovery that you did not at all. the interruption is the important part—without it you skip over the gaps and never find out where they were.

- **Write it down.** a thought in your head feels vague and unclear. sometimes as a fact and undeniable. on paper it becomes clear, arguable. sometimes you start seeing the patterns of your thoughts, journaling can even help updating those patterns for more healthy ones.

- **Wait five seconds.** between the feeling and the response. not to calm down, just to delay. the automatic read arrives instantly and the checking arrives a beat later, and most of my worst responses went out in that gap. the delay doesn't make the feeling correct or false. it just means i'm not acting on the first version of it.

- **Run small premortems.** this one comes from gary klein, who studied decision-making in fire crews and emergency rooms and found that asking "what went wrong" afterward almost always comes too late—by then everyone has the story. so you do it in advance instead imagining you already finished the important task you are planning. if this goes badly, where exactly does it go badly? for example, before a difficult conversation the answer is usually something like i'll get defensive when she brings up that timeline, and having that sentence ready changes what happens when she actually does.

- **Don't treat friction as a verdict.** feeling strained while learning something isn't proof you aren't capable of it. the methods that work best; testing yourself before rereading, spacing things out, mixing topics. these all feel worse while you're doing them, which is why most people don't use them. researchers call these desirable difficulties. that doesn't mean every struggle is useful. sometimes an explanation is just bad, or you're tired. it means the feeling can't tell you which, so don't let it decide.

## Metacognition vs Overthinking

Metacognition is used everywhere, especially in high stake places. leaders auditing a strategy before the market does it for them. athletes' abilities to notice, monitor, instead and adjusting their thinking during the game, under pressure. it's also important in what people call agency—being the one making decisions and controlling their lives instead of being controlled by it.

And it isn't overthinking. overthinking is running the same thought again and again, feeding itself. it feels like working on the problem but it's just producing more of it. metacognition is stepping out and looking at the loop instead of adding another lap; why does this keep coming back, what is it protecting, has it ever been right.

I still don't have it easily. the automatic read shows up as convincing as it always did, and most days i notice it after i've already acted on it. the difference is that i notice at all now, which is more than nothing.
