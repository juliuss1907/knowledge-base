# An all too common investment story

**Author:** The Intellectual Edge
**Source:** Substack
**Published:** 2026-07-12

---

## The Story of Julian

Julian is a keen individual investor with a few years of experience and good returns. He works full-time as a product designer. Since he lacks time for exhaustive stock research, he subscribes to investment newsletters and uses Claude AI to research ideas.

One Sunday morning, both his favorite newsletters pitched the same stock (BTC Corp). But one presented a compelling **buy** recommendation, while the other presented an equally compelling **sell** recommendation.

This sparked uncomfortable cognitive dissonance. Any other week, he'd have been persuaded by the language and the promise of profit. But now, knowing one investor had to be wrong, he approached both with far more skepticism.

## The Insight

Had Julian received only one email — say, from the long investor — he'd have gone long BTC Corp. Had he only received the short investor's email, he'd have gone short.

A stock pitch is a product of the investors' unique perspectives, knowledge, and biases. It's not gospel. It's a game of opinion — Julian had just stopped treating it as such.

He realized he had been **outsourcing all of his own thinking:**

- When companies released news, he didn't check the company website — he relied on people on the internet to package everything for him
- Every bullish argument likely had an equally compelling bearish argument against it
- **"In fooling himself into thinking he was doing his own research, he fooled himself into thinking he knew what he held."**
- All he knew was someone else's opinion on his holdings, not his own

## The Lesson

Across Substack, Twitter, and research platforms, we're inundated with "golden tickets" and "sure things." It starts to feel like every stock is a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity. Being barraged by ideas makes it easy to forget to find ideas of our own.

Key realizations:
- **Statistics don't lie:** More than half of drivers think they're above average. We can't all beat the market. Only a few investors are truly extraordinary.
- **In outsourcing thinking, we give up something unique: ourselves.** You have knowledge and perspectives others don't — you give that up when you leave research to someone else.
- **Each investor has gaps:** The long investor missed the accounting flaw the short investor knows. The short investor missed the growth avenue the long investor spent years studying.

## The Difference: Following vs. Investing Alongside

> "If you're to follow someone into something, you have to be fully aware of their reasons, so much so that you are not following them into it at all, but rather investing in the same thing as them. The difference is subtle but important."

External research is **supplementary** to your own — it is not yours. When you take research seriously, you stop following people and start leveraging their knowledge to benefit your own.

## Beware of Persuasion

If you don't research ideas for yourself, everything becomes a matter of persuasion. A stock pitch becomes less about merit and more about salesmanship. It's like going to a mechanic with a car you know nothing about — with enough scary words, you'll spend thousands on unnecessary parts.

Without knowing what you're doing, you become convinced not by the right thing, but by the **first thing.** Read the bullish argument first → skeptical of the bearish. Read the bearish first → skeptical of the bullish.

## The Core Principle

> "Only when it's far too late will you realize how fragile borrowed opinions are."

Remember: you're buying from someone who wants to sell, or selling to someone who wants to buy. You're making a bold claim that you disagree with the other person so much that you're willing to put hard-earned money on it. A bet like this deserves extremely careful thought and research.

**Know what you own. Use research platforms as a supplement, not a crutch.**

## Bonus Note

> "Anything that is accepted wisdom is highly dangerous. What is profitable is rarely what is commonly discussed. Common and superior profit potential are not synonymous. It is the unknown things that offer high profit — and they offer it because of the very fact it is unknown."
